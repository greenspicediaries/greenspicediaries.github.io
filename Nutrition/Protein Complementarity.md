# Protein Complementarity

## Idea
Individual plant proteins are low in different essential amino acids, so pairing grains with legumes covers the gaps — which is exactly what traditional cuisines were already doing long before anyone measured it.

## Definition
Protein quality depends on the full set of nine essential amino acids. Cereals are relatively low in lysine; legumes are relatively low in methionine. Eaten together they complement each other and the combined profile is close to complete. Rice and dal, roti and chana, idli and sambar, tortilla and beans, hummus and pita all encode this. The older framing that the pairing had to happen in the same meal has been superseded — the body draws on a circulating amino-acid pool, so what matters is total intake and variety across the day.

## Source
Frances Moore Lappé's *Diet for a Small Planet* (1971) popularised the idea; the same-meal requirement was later walked back, and the current position is reflected in the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics' position paper on vegetarian diets.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is the nutritional argument underneath traditional grain-and-legume cuisines, and it belongs to the broader pattern that plant-based eating works by combination rather than by any single hero ingredient — the same logic as [[Umami Without Meat]].

**Paths** — *where this leads*
It gives a high-protein vegetarian plate a principle rather than a protein powder: build around a legume, add a grain, and the macro takes care of itself. It also makes the familiar thali or khichdi defensible as *already* optimised, which is a much stronger position than retrofitting Indian food to a Western macro template — a point that runs straight into [[Cultural Specificity Is an Asset]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It sits beside protein density and bioavailability as the other two things that actually matter in plant protein, and next to [[Satiety Over Calories]], since legume-and-grain meals score well on fullness for reasons beyond their amino acids.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
The complementarity framing is often oversold: total protein quantity and leucine content matter more for most people than amino-acid pairing, and plant protein is also less bioavailable, so volume genuinely has to be higher. The strict same-meal version of the rule is simply outdated and repeating it adds anxiety without adding accuracy.
