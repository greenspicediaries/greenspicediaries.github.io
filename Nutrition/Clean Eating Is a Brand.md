# Clean Eating Is a Brand

## Idea
"Clean eating" is a marketing category with no scientific or regulatory definition, so it tells you about the seller's story, not the food's effect on your body.

## Definition
No food agency anywhere defines "clean." The phrase started as 1990s bodybuilding slang for lean, unprocessed eating, was carried into the mainstream by Tosca Reno's *The Eat-Clean Diet* (2007), and then grew into a multi-billion-dollar label on social media. Because the word is undefined, it is unfalsifiable: anyone can call anything clean, and nobody can be wrong. The label also smuggles in a class problem, since the foods that get called clean (organic, specialty, imported) often cost double, which turns a budget constraint into an imagined moral failure. The useful replacement is specific: eat in a way that works for your body, your budget and your life, and name actual properties (fibre, protein, how processed) instead of borrowing an adjective.

## Source
Tosca Reno, *The Eat-Clean Diet* (2007) popularised the term; its bodybuilding and 1960s-70s natural-foods lineage predates her. Researched from the Green Spice Diaries "What eating clean gets wrong" video.

## Compass

**Roots:** *where this comes from*
It is a named instance of [[The Health Halo Effect]]: "clean" is a halo word that does the judging before any looking happens, and the moralised vocabulary (clean implies dirty) is where the guilt economy of food begins.

**Paths:** *where this leads*
Dropping the label forces better questions, the same ones behind [[Satiety Over Calories]]: what does this food actually do, does it keep me full, what does it cost me. That specificity is what lets a [[Healthified Indian Classic]] be evaluated on its merits rather than on vibes.

**Neighbors:** *what lives nearby*
It sits beside [[Healthy Is Not a Permission Slip]], which handles the other half of the moralising, where a virtuous label licenses overconsumption. Both are about labels substituting for attention.

**Clash:** *what pushes against this*
The impulse behind clean eating is not wrong: more whole foods and fewer ultra-processed ones is directionally good advice. The failure is in the packaging of that advice as a binary identity, so the critique has to dismantle the label without discarding the direction.
