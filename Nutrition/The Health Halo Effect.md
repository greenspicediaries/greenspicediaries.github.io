# The Health Halo Effect

## Idea
A food with a virtuous story (natural, ancient, traditional) gets judged healthier than the evidence supports, because the story does the evaluating instead of the nutrition.

## Definition
The health halo is a documented cognitive bias: one positive cue about a food spills over into every other judgement about it. In the canonical experiments, people estimated a Subway meal at roughly 150 fewer calories than a comparable McDonald's meal, and rated the identical sandwich lighter when it wore the "healthy" brand's wrapper. The same mechanism runs through home kitchens: ghee earns a halo from Ayurveda that butter never gets, even though both are concentrated dairy fat in the same cardiovascular category, and jaggery or coconut sugar earn one from tradition even though, at the teaspoon quantities actually used, they behave like sugar. The halo does not make a food unhealthy. It makes the label arrive before the looking.

## Source
Formalised by Pierre Chandon and Brian Wansink, "The Biasing Health Halos of Fast-Food Restaurant Health Claims," *Journal of Consumer Research* (2007); the phrase circulated in 1990s food journalism and descends from Thorndike's 1920 halo effect. Surfaced for this garden by the Green Spice Diaries "Good vs Bad Food" video research.

## Compass

**Roots:** *where this comes from*
It is a special case of the general halo effect from social psychology, applied to the plate, and it is the mechanism underneath [[Healthy Is Not a Permission Slip]]: the halo is what issues the permission in the first place.

**Paths:** *where this leads*
Naming the bias changes the question from "is this food good or bad" to "what is this food actually giving me," which is the working stance behind every [[Healthified Indian Classic]]. It also flags words like natural, ancient and traditional as claims to check rather than verdicts to accept, the move [[Clean Eating Is a Brand]] makes about one specific label.

**Neighbors:** *what lives nearby*
It lives beside [[Satiety Over Calories]], since both argue that the body's accounting and the story's accounting diverge, and beside marketing-built categories generally, where packaging does the persuading.

**Clash:** *what pushes against this*
Some halos point at real differences: ghee genuinely wins on smoke point and lactose, and whole foods genuinely beat ultra-processed ones on average, so treating every traditional claim as bias would overcorrect. The halo is a reason to verify, not a reason to invert.
