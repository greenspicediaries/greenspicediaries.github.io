# Pantry as Infrastructure

## Idea
A well-chosen pantry is not storage — it is the fixed layer that makes spontaneous cooking possible, because it converts "what can I make" from a shopping problem into an assembly problem.

## Definition
Treating the pantry as infrastructure means stocking a deliberate, stable set of shelf-stable multipliers — whole spices, lentils and beans, grains, tinned tomatoes, tahini, nut butters, vinegars, soy and miso, dried chillies, aromatics — chosen because each one unlocks many dishes rather than one. The test is combinatorial: an ingredient earns its shelf space by how many meals it participates in. With that layer fixed, a weekly shop only has to supply fresh produce and the gap is bridged by what is already there.

## Source
A through-line in pantry-first cooking writing, from Tamar Adler's *An Everlasting Meal* to the stocked-kitchen chapters of most Indian home-cooking manuals.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It belongs to the same front-loading logic as [[Mise en Place]] and [[The Sunday Reset]] — invest once, remove friction many times — and its contents are shaped directly by [[Whole vs Ground Spices]], since the whole-spice tier is what gives the drawer its long shelf life.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
A combinatorial pantry is what makes [[Leftover Makeover]] possible, because rescuing an odd half-bowl of rice requires that the sauce ingredients are already in the house. It also quietly determines the ceiling on [[Umami Without Meat]]: miso, dried shiitake and nutritional yeast are pantry decisions, not recipe decisions.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It is the shelf-stable counterpart to the [[Two-Cook-Window Strategy]]'s fridge components — one layer lasts months, the other days, and good weeks use both. It also rhymes with [[Constraints Create Creativity]], since a fixed pantry is a constraint that generates variety rather than limiting it.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Pantries rot in a different way: an over-stocked shelf becomes a graveyard of single-use ingredients bought for one recipe, and ground spices quietly expire while looking fine. It also assumes space, money up front, and a stable kitchen, none of which is universal.
