# Borrowed Technique, Native Flavour

## Idea
The most interesting fusion keeps a foreign *technique* and fills it with familiar flavour, rather than the reverse — because technique travels cleanly while flavour carries identity.

## Definition
A technique is a mechanism: rice-paper wrapping, phyllo layering, pan-frying a stacked potato terrine, cold-fermenting a high-hydration dough. Mechanisms are portable because they make no claim about what goes inside them. Flavour, by contrast, is where a dish's cultural identity lives. Pairing a borrowed mechanism with native flavour therefore produces something that feels new but tastes coherent — rice-paper samosa rolls, paneer naan pizza, phyllo with a spiced spinach filling. Reversed, the result usually reads as a watered-down version of both.

## Source
A recurring pattern in diaspora and immigrant cooking, discussed in food writing on culinary adaptation; visible in practice across Indian-inspired recreations of East Asian and Mediterranean formats.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It depends on recognising which half of a dish is mechanism and which is identity, and it inherits from [[Indian Food Is Not One Cuisine]] — a tradition that has absorbed Persian, Portuguese and Central Asian techniques for centuries is already proof that technique travels.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
It is a reliable idea generator: take any crisp or wrapped or layered format and ask what the Indian filling version is, and you have a dish that is novel in form and familiar in taste. That combination is precisely what makes a [[Healthified Indian Classic]] work as content, because the hook is in the form while the comfort is in the flavour.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It is the cross-border twin of [[Cultural Specificity Is an Asset]] — one says keep the flavour specific, the other says the wrapper can come from anywhere — and it sits beside substitution logic like [[Every Cuisine Has a Souring Agent]].

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
The line between adaptation and flattening is genuinely contested, and a technique swap can quietly remove the thing that made a dish itself — rice-paper samosa rolls are not samosas, and claiming otherwise is the part people object to. Some techniques also carry their flavour with them, so the clean separation does not always hold.
