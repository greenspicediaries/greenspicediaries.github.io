# Every Cuisine Has a Souring Agent

## Idea
Acid is a structural requirement in cooking, not a regional preference, so every cuisine has independently settled on local souring agents — and they are not freely interchangeable.

## Definition
Acid cuts fat, brightens dull flavour, balances sweetness and makes salt read as seasoning rather than saltiness. Because the need is universal and the ingredients were local, every cuisine arrived at its own answers: tamarind, kokum, amchur, lime, yoghurt and tomato in India; vinegar and wine in Europe; lime and tomatillo in Mexico; rice vinegar and citrus in East Asia; sumac and pomegranate molasses in the Levant. They differ not only in strength but in what they bring alongside the acid — sweetness, astringency, fruit, fermentation — which is why swapping one for another changes the dish rather than merely adjusting it.

## Source
Samin Nosrat's *Salt, Fat, Acid, Heat* makes the universality argument; the regional inventories come from comparative cuisine writing.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It belongs to the same family as [[Salt Early, Salt Often]] — both are seasoning variables that get treated as garnishes — and it is one of the clearest pieces of evidence for [[Indian Food Is Not One Cuisine]], since the souring agent changes every few hundred kilometres.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
It gives a reliable diagnostic: a dish that tastes flat, heavy or somehow unfinished usually wants acid, not more salt or more spice. It is also the standard corrective for over-stacked [[Umami Without Meat]], where the savoury monotone needs cutting rather than deepening.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It sits beside fat and salt as the third great balancing lever, and it rhymes with [[Borrowed Technique, Native Flavour]] — substituting lime for kokum is exactly the kind of cross-border swap that works technically while shifting the dish's identity.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Acid is also the easiest thing to overdo, and an over-soured dish is harder to rescue than an under-seasoned one. Several souring agents also behave badly under heat — yoghurt splits, lime turns bitter when boiled — so the universal need does not translate into a universal method.
