# Tenderise, Then Sear

## Idea
Splitting cooking into two stages, a gentle par-cook for the inside and a violent sear for the outside, lets each surface get exactly the heat it needs instead of one compromise temperature.

## Definition
A raw, wet vegetable in a hot pan spends its time steaming at 100°C while the centre stays raw. Par-cooking first (boiling a whole cauliflower fork-tender, blanching broccoli, simmering potatoes) finishes the interior and softens the surface, so the second stage only has to dry and brown a thin outer layer. That is why the sear then happens fast and deep: browning reactions need a dry surface well above the boiling point of water, and the par-cook has already paid the water bill. The pattern is everywhere once named: boiled-then-roasted crispy potatoes, blanched-then-charred corn, steamed-then-fried dumplings, a boiled cauliflower sliced into steaks and charred in a skillet.

## Source
Standard professional two-stage cookery (blanch, then finish); the science framing popularised by J. Kenji López-Alt in *The Food Lab* (2015), most famously the boil-then-roast potato. Mined from the Green Spice Diaries cauliflower steak and charred corn videos.

## Compass

**Roots:** *where this comes from*
It exists because the [[Maillard Reaction]] has a temperature floor that wet food cannot reach, which makes this a staged application of [[Crisp Is the Absence of Water]].

**Paths:** *where this leads*
The two stages can be separated in time, which turns the technique into a meal-prep move: par-cook on Sunday, sear in the five minutes before dinner, the engine inside [[Cook Once, Eat Twice]] for anything that must arrive crisp.

**Neighbors:** *what lives nearby*
It rhymes with [[Tempering]], where a separate hot-fat stage is also added so one pot does not have to do two jobs, and with the chilled-then-fried tikki, which stages moisture instead of doneness.

**Clash:** *what pushes against this*
Two stages cost a pot, a tray and time, and delicate vegetables (zucchini, mushrooms) overshoot from tender to waterlogged in the first stage, so the technique suits dense, slow-centred things. One-stage roasting at high heat often gets close enough for a weeknight.
