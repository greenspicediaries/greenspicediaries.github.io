# Umami Without Meat

## Idea
The savoury depth that meat supplies comes from free glutamates and nucleotides, and plenty of plant ingredients are rich in both, so vegetarian food tastes thin only when the cook has not gone looking for them.

## Definition
Umami is the taste of free glutamate, amplified sharply when nucleotides (inosinate, guanylate) are present alongside it. Meat and fish are convenient concentrations of both, but tomato, mushroom (especially dried shiitake), fermented soy, miso, nutritional yeast, aged cheese, seaweed, roasted alliums, and anything deeply browned all carry significant amounts. Because glutamate and nucleotides multiply rather than add, combining two sources produces far more savoury depth than doubling either one.

## Source
Kikunae Ikeda's identification of glutamate (1908) and the later work on nucleotide synergy; the practical cooking version is in McGee's *On Food and Cooking*.

## Compass

**Roots:** *where this comes from*
It answers the main objection to plant-based cooking and sits underneath the whole project of [[Protein Complementarity]], since both are cases where plants work by combination rather than by any single ingredient doing everything.

**Paths:** *where this leads*
Treating umami as a budget to be stacked turns the vague advice "add more flavour" into a shopping list, and the single cheapest line on it is browning, which is why the [[Maillard Reaction]] does so much of the work. Stacking also explains why dishes like a mushroom-and-lentil ragù or a tomato-and-white-bean bake land as satisfying rather than virtuous.

**Neighbors:** *what lives nearby*
It belongs beside the other depth-building moves ([[Fat-Soluble Flavour]] for aroma, acid for lift, [[Salt Early, Salt Often]] for seasoning), and fermentation sits alongside it as a way of manufacturing glutamate rather than sourcing it.

**Clash:** *what pushes against this*
Stacked umami gets heavy fast: too many savoury sources at once produces a flat, brothy monotone that needs acid and freshness to cut it. Several of the strongest sources are also high in sodium, which runs straight into the sodium ceiling that [[Salt Early, Salt Often]] is trying to respect.
