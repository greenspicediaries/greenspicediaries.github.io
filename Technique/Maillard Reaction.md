# Maillard Reaction

## Idea
Browning is not cooking food more — it is a chemical reaction between amino acids and sugars that manufactures hundreds of new aroma compounds that were not in the raw ingredient at all.

## Definition
Above roughly 140°C, amino acids and reducing sugars on a food's surface react and cascade into a huge family of new molecules — nutty, roasted, meaty, bready. It is distinct from caramelisation, which is sugar alone. The practical constraint is water: as long as the surface is wet it stays near 100°C and cannot brown, which is why drying the surface and giving food room in the pan matters more than turning the heat up.

## Source
Named for Louis-Camille Maillard (1912); the cook's account is in McGee's *On Food and Cooking* and Nik Sharma's *The Flavor Equation*.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It belongs to the same family of ideas as [[Tempering]] — both are short, high-heat windows that create aroma rather than merely transferring it — and it is governed entirely by the fact that [[Crisp Is the Absence of Water]].

**Paths** — *where this leads*
It is the single biggest lever for [[Umami Without Meat]], because roasting vegetables, searing paneer or tofu, and browning onions is how a vegetarian dish gets the savoury depth that meat would otherwise supply. It also rewrites technique: a crowded pan is not a minor sin but a guarantee of steamed, grey food.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It sits next to caramelisation, which it is constantly confused with, and next to the enzymatic browning that turns a cut apple brown without any heat at all. In the kitchen it shares a decision space with [[Salt Early, Salt Often]], since salt drawn to the surface pulls out the very water that browning needs gone.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Browning competes with gentle cooking: the heat that develops aroma also drives off the volatile freshness of herbs and delicate spices, so a dish can be deeply browned and simultaneously flat. Pushed too far it crosses into acrid, and the high-heat/high-char end of the range carries real nutritional caveats around acrylamide and advanced glycation end-products.
