# Garam Masala Is a Finishing Spice

## Idea
Garam masala is a blend of aromatics that are already toasted and ground, so adding it at the beginning of a dish destroys the very volatiles it exists to contribute.

## Definition
Unlike turmeric or chilli, which are pigment-and-heat spices that survive long cooking, garam masala is built from highly volatile aromatics — cinnamon, clove, cardamom, mace, black pepper — that have already been toasted and ground. Their aroma compounds are fragile and water-soluble enough to steam off during a simmer. Added in the last minute or two, off the heat, the blend perfumes the dish; added at the start, it mostly disappears and leaves a vague woody dullness behind.

## Source
Standard practice in North Indian home cooking, frequently inverted by Western recipe writing that treats garam masala as a generic curry powder.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
This is the clearest consequence of [[Whole vs Ground Spices]] — the blend is pre-ground, so its clock started long before it reached the pan — and it depends on understanding which spices in a dish are structural and which are perfume.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Once you sort your spice drawer into *build* spices and *finish* spices, the order of a recipe becomes something you can reason about instead of memorise, which feeds directly into [[Cooking by Signal, Not by Timer]]. It also makes garam masala a correction tool: a dish that tastes finished but flat often needs aroma at the end rather than more salt.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It is the spice-drawer version of adding fresh herbs off the heat, and it works in the same window as a finishing [[Tempering]] — both are late additions that put aroma back on top of a dish that has already been built.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Some dishes genuinely want the blend early, where long braising is meant to round those aromatics into the background rather than showcase them, and a few regional blends are robust enough to take it. The rule is also easy to over-apply into timing anxiety, when the honest version is simply: later is usually better than earlier.
