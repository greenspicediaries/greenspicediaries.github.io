# Tempering

## Idea
Spices are bloomed in hot fat at the start or end of a dish to convert raw, dusty aroma into round, developed flavour that then carries through everything it touches.

## Definition
Tempering (*tadka*, *chhonk*, *phoron*, *oggarane* depending on where in India you are standing) is the act of heating fat, adding whole or ground spices in a deliberate order, and cooking them for seconds until their aroma changes. The hot fat does two jobs at once: it pulls fat-soluble aroma compounds out of the spice, and it pushes the spice past its raw stage without burning it. The tempering is then either the foundation a dish is built on or a finishing spoonful poured over the top.

## Source
Foundational across every Indian regional cuisine; the food-science explanation of why it works is the extraction of fat-soluble volatiles, described in Nik Sharma's *The Flavor Equation* and McGee's *On Food and Cooking*.

## Compass

**Roots:** *where this comes from*
Tempering only makes sense once you accept that most spice aroma is [[Fat-Soluble Flavour]] and simply will not come out in water, which is why nearly every Indian dish opens with fat rather than liquid. It is also the clearest case of [[Indian Food Is Not One Cuisine]]: the same move carries a different name, fat, and spice order in every region.

**Paths:** *where this leads*
Learn to read the aroma change and you stop needing recipe timings at all, which is the beginning of [[Cooking by Signal, Not by Timer]]. It also unlocks the finishing tempering as a repair tool: a flat dal or a dull bowl of vegetables can be rescued at the end with thirty seconds of hot fat and cumin, which makes it a quiet ally of [[Leftover Makeover]].

**Neighbors:** *what lives nearby*
It is the same underlying move as blooming tomato paste or toasting curry powder in a European or Thai kitchen, and it rhymes with the [[Maillard Reaction]] in that both are short windows where heat creates aroma that was not there before. The decision of whether to temper whole seeds or ground powder is really the question behind [[Whole vs Ground Spices]].

**Clash:** *what pushes against this*
The window is brutally short. Mustard seeds want to pop and cumin wants to darken, but ground spices can scorch in under five seconds and burnt spice is bitter in a way no amount of salt or acid will fix. It also pushes directly against low-oil cooking, since the fat is not a garnish here but the extraction medium, which is a real tension in any [[Healthified Indian Classic]].
